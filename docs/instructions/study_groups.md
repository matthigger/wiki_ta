# Teaching

Communicating anything gracefully, mathematics and software included, is hard to do!  I most certainly am not done figuring it out.  These are a few ideas which I've found productive in my own practice.  Reading this little document certainly won't make you a better teacher, but maybe it plants a few idea-seeds which your experience with students grows into stronger teaching habits.  Consider trying out some ideas below and let your students reactions inform your teaching.  There's more than one way to teach well and observing your students carefully provides the evidence you'll need to develop into your own best teaching-self.

## People feel first and think second

This morning (sept 2026) I got a phone call from an HVAC company trying to collect on an invoice despite them not having finished the job.  It wasn't a pleasant call.  When I went to work afterwards I found myself distracted, frustrated and cloudy.  I was cutting with a dull knife.

**People do their best thinking when they feel good**.  Ideally students are well fed, rested and haven't spoken to any HVAC companies threatening about collection agencies recently.  Of course, as an instructor you can't do anything about what emotional energy they bring to the table, but we can shape and nurture what shows up.  Jokes, a smile, a genuine greeting all go a long way.  In group settings, encouraging students to engage with each other does similar work.

For a student to ask a question and draw attention to their own misunderstanding requires a level of trust and comfort which are derived from an instructor's relationship with the student, not their expertise in the subject.

## We learn the most when we work the hardest (and still succeed)

Much like goldilocks' porridge, there is a sweet spot to challenging a student to maximize their learning.  Ask a question too easy or hard and their skills are stagnant because they've succeeded too quickly or couldn't make meaningful progress and have given up.  (Too hard, in particular, is troublesome because it dents confidence, which is often lost more quickly than its gained).  **Ideally, a student should work on a problem at the furthest reaches of their capabilities, and succeed** (see also: claude add a link to zone of proximal dvevlopment on wikipedia).  You can tell a student is working on a "just-right" problem because they produce sustained effective effort.  

Towards this end, **a teacher should provide as little support to a student as possible, such that they still succeed**.  
At the end of a problem a student can look back on the path their minds took to get there, rising above this particular context and generalizing their knowledge to the point where its useful in future problems.  In the middle of a problem, before its complete, their skills haven't ``got it" yet.  We should provide supports to students (hints, advice, or maybe draw their attention to a relevant warm-up problem) such that they are successful.

On the other hand, there's no "free lunch" in learning.  Offer too much support and the problem is so easy that the resulting skills languish.  This begs the question, how do we find goldilock's sweet spot, offering just enough support for success and no more?  **Shut up, and listen to your students.**  Give them some space to try a problem or idea first, and see how they respond.  In whole class instruction I often ask a warm up question and get them talking just to activate whatever ideas they have ahead of time before I take the lead and develop an idea for them.  In small study groups on HW problems, a TA leader could ask students questions so they can isolate the particular trouble they're having.  This sets the expectation that the student lead the discussion towards some insight and allows the instructor to listen for what ideas might need developing, queuing them to offer a little support where needed.  So long as a student is making forward progress on a problem, it may be best to just get out of the way.  (This is often true in study group settings)

## The attention bargain: when a student gives attention, how much learning do they get?

At its core, students are swapping their attention and effort for some knowledge or skills.  What's the exchange rate that they get?  Should a student show up and listen to you for 5 minutes, what do they get out of it?  If our instruction is not "just-right" for a student (see above), or we ramble on about some unrelated thing, or even if the administrative details take up too much time, students will notice that they're not getting a good deal.  At some point, they'll stop spending their attention and effort for the skills we're trading.  As best you can, **maximize how much learning you can give your students for their attention**.  In whole class instruction, this often means setting out to study ideas which are "just-right" for as many students as possible (no small feat when capabilities vary among students and we don't get to check in individually a priori!).  In smaller group settings, its helpful to identify what motivates individual students: do they have an apetite for some nuanced explanation of some subtlety or are they trying to get an A with as little effort as possible?  Trim the extras for the second student and, should you offer some new ideas to the first, ensure that the time and effort it takes to explain justifies their value. 

## Study Group Specific Tips:
#### Encourage students to help each other.  
- Students often have an easier time asking peers for help than asking someone with more authority (TA or instructor).
- Supporting students re-enforce their own undrestanding (or identify holes) by having [to talk out loud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubber_duck_debugging)
- You can't help everybody at once, that expectation can create ``busy" days for you where students are waiting

#### Don't re-teach anything.
Minimize "whole-group" instruction; it is not productive for students who picked it up in class. 
If you suspect everybody has the same issue, you can offer a quick (<5 mins) intervention, but see how much of it you can get the students to lead themselves.

#### Reference course materials.
There's different conventions/notations and entirely different approaches out there whose quality varies.  There's often a reason we approach a topic in one way or another.  Read our class notes so you can sync up on how we tackle a topic.  Avoid referencing approaches or materials generated outside of the course, or run them by me if you've got something you think is an improvement so I can benefit too (I learned of a way to represent Dijkstra's Algorithm from a TA, its now my in-class approach to the topic).
